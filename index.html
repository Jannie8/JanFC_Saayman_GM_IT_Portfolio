Introduction

My name is Jan Frederik Cronje Saayman (but just Jan is fine 😊). I am 21 years old and currently studying for my Bachelor of Computing in Software Engineering at Belgium Campus iTversity in Pretoria, South Africa. Since childhood, I’ve always been curious about technology — I was the kid with all the gadgets — but at the same time I’ve kept a strong love for the outdoors and nature.

Alongside my studies, I founded Jan’s Tutoring, a business that started as a way to save money for travel but has since grown into a team offering both in-person and online tutoring across Pretoria. This venture not only allowed me to make my dream of traveling through Europe a reality, but also taught me valuable lessons about leadership, teamwork, and responsibility.

With my love for Europe, my passion for learning, and my interest in global collaboration, I joined the Global Acting in IT minor. This program gives me the opportunity to expand my IT capabilities while working with international peers, learning from their knowledge, and experiencing their cultures.

My goals for this program are not only to strengthen my technical skills — particularly in prototyping and data visualization — but also to grow personally, by developing confidence in teamwork and building intercultural awareness.

Future Tech – Eindhoven

Online Week

Null-Measurement

Before the program began, I had some general knowledge about global IT topics such as IoT, AI, and data science, but I had never worked on a real-world project with such an international team. My understanding of Future Tech was mostly academic — I knew about immersive technologies and their applications in gaming, but not how they could connect to sustainability challenges.

During the online week, each day introduced us to different domains we would later explore in depth. On Monday, we met all the lecturers from the five participating universities. They explained the minor, introduced us to the Hartbeespoort Dam hyacinth project, and gave us our first taste of SDGs and water quality. Already here, I realised how little I actually knew about water quality monitoring and the scale of the hyacinth problem.

Throughout the week we also had introductory lectures:

  • UX Design (Monday)
  • Intercultural Competence (Tuesday)
  • Future Tech and IoT (Tuesday)
  • AI (Wednesday)
  • Personal Development (Wednesday)
  • Data Science (Thursday)

Each session deepened my vision of how these technologies could connect to our project. For example, before Tuesday I never considered IoT sensor networks as a way to manage ecosystems, but by the end of the session I could already see how IoT could support real-time water quality monitoring at Hartbeespoort.

By the end of the online week, my initial vision of technology had shifted: Future Tech is not just about tools and gadgets — it is about combining innovation with sustainability and working together across borders to make an impact.

Project Contribution

This was also the week when our project teams were formed. I was assigned to a group with Jana (Austria), Tamerlan (Belgium), and Georgi (Netherlands). We quickly connected and named ourselves Four Elements: Jana as Earth, Tamerlan as Water, Georgi as Air, and me as Fire.

Our first assignment was a team-building task: we had to get to know each other, create a brand identity, and present ourselves to the lecturers and peers. My personal contribution was to define our core values and team goals, making sure they reflected both our cultural diversity and our project ambitions. This role gave me the chance to take initiative early on and set a foundation for how we wanted to collaborate.

In addition, we spent time brainstorming how technologies like immersive tools, IoT, and AI might fit into the Hartbeespoort project. Although our ideas were rough at this stage, they laid the groundwork for the concepts we would develop in Eindhoven.

Cultural Awareness

The online week was also my first real exposure to intercultural teamwork. We came from very different backgrounds and countries, and already small things like communication styles and pacing showed differences. During the Intercultural Competence workshop, we received scenarios and had to decide as a group which outcomes we preferred. These discussions revealed how values can differ across cultures — for example, some members leaned toward collective decision-making, while others pushed for efficiency.

Socially, we also began to build friendships despite working online. Even through screens, we found humor in our cultural quirks and started forming trust. This early bonding made me feel more confident about traveling abroad to meet my team in person.

Week 2 – Deepening the Work

Null-Measurement

This changed as the week progressed. The guest lecture from Aquality introduced us to a real-world IoT solution: an autonomous sensor boat that collects water quality data while navigating across a lake. This showed me how IoT could be combined with mapping to deliver practical, visual insights to stakeholders. Similarly, our visit to Enversed VR demonstrated how AR and VR can support industries beyond entertainment. I realised that immersive simulations could be used for public awareness campaigns or to train decision-makers before implementing costly interventions.

By midweek, during our excursion to Amsterdam, I gained new insights into the cultural side of water management. At This is Holland, I learned how the Netherlands’ long history of managing water has shaped both its landscapes and its culture. This gave me context for understanding how Dutch stakeholders might approach our project. My vision of Future Tech expanded to include not just innovation and prototypes, but also the heritage and cultural values that shape how solutions are received and used.

Project Contribution

This week was heavily focused on developing our project concepts. With Maike’s guidance, we identified our primary stakeholders and wrote three problem statements for each. We then brainstormed potential solutions, categorising them into tech, human, and data approaches. My contribution included researching case studies on IoT sensor networks and exploring how they could provide real-time dashboards of water quality. This research fed directly into our later group concepts.

During Ferry’s design process workshop, we used prompt cards like sound and rolling to inspire out-of-the-box ideas. Our team created a virtual Hartbeespoort map where pollution increased when people made noise (sound input) and was cleaned when a ball was rolled (rolling interaction). We added IoT sensors as part of the design, linking the concept back to our project. I actively contributed to this brainstorming by suggesting ways to connect the inputs and outputs, ensuring the game not only entertained but also raised awareness about the dam’s condition.

Throughout the week, we worked on refining three potential concepts. My role was to provide technical insights into which IoT approaches were feasible, while also contributing to the design of visual posters for our Monday presentation the following week.

Whether at the hostel bar, exploring Amsterdam’s nightlife, or simply talking with my team, I grew more comfortable navigating intercultural friendships. UX Design – Lleida, Spain

Week 1 – Arrival in Lleida & First Impressions

Null-Measurement

Before arriving in Spain, my understanding of UX Design was mostly theoretical — I knew it was about user-centred design and interface layout, but I hadn’t yet experienced how culture, language, and real people’s emotions influence technology. I wanted to learn how to translate empathy into design.

Our journey from the Netherlands to Spain already felt like a lesson in adaptation. After wrapping up in Eindhoven, Marco, Rourke, Musti and I flew to Barcelona and decided to stay the night before travelling to Lleida. Near the airport we stumbled upon a festive fair — lights, music, food stalls, laughter — and quickly realised a national holiday was starting. Hungry, we wandered until we spotted an African restaurant. We couldn’t resist; we missed home. The restaurant’s quirky “African-themed” decor gave us a good laugh, but the people were warm and welcoming. The owner served pizza and beer while chatting to us for almost two hours. For a brief moment, in the middle of Spain, it felt like home.

The next morning, our travel plan fell apart — we missed the one-hour bus and had to take a three-hour train instead. Yet it turned into an unexpected highlight. The train hugged the coast from Barcelona to Tarragona, sunlight bouncing off the sea while we watched the waves roll by. It was peaceful and grounding — the kind of moment that makes you grateful to be young and exploring the world.

When we finally arrived in Lleida, the city immediately felt different from everywhere else we’d been. It wasn’t a big tourist place; it was authentic, quiet, and local. Hardly anyone spoke English, which meant every conversation required creativity — a mix of gestures, smiles, and Google Translate.

That Monday was a Spanish public holiday. I started the day with a 5 km run through the old streets and ended up at Castell de la Suda, perched on a hill with a panoramic view of Lleida. That run became my personal ritual for clearing my head. Later, Alexa took us to another fair in town, where we ate, went on carnival rides, and ended the night laughing like kids. It was the perfect welcome to Spain.

Project Contribution

Classes at the Universitat de Lleida started at 15:00 and ended at 19:00 — a rhythm that first felt strange, but soon fit perfectly into the long, sunny evenings.

Our lecturer, Toni, introduced the User-Centred Design (UX) module. He explained that UX Design is not about making something look pretty — it’s about creating experiences that are meaningful, usable, and inclusive. His examples of tools designed for people with disabilities completely shifted my mindset: design isn’t just visual; it’s human. We also met Rosa, who outlined the course expectations and guided us through the assessment process.

Toni then paired each international team with a local Spanish student to bridge language and cultural gaps. That’s how Carme joined Four Elements — and she turned out to be an amazing addition. She taught us Spanish phrases, helped us translate signs, and brought humour to every discussion.

That afternoon, each group presented their project from the Netherlands. We explained our Hartbeespoort Dam concept and its potential for environmental monitoring. It felt great to see how far we had come since Eindhoven. To celebrate, Jana and I stuck to tradition — Licor 43 with milk for everyone.

On Wednesday, we had our first deep dive into UX tools. Toni introduced wireframing, and we began our first prototype assignment: to create a digital dashboard for our project using any platform of our choice. We decided on Figma, thinking it would be simple. We were wrong — at first. Dragging boxes and connecting buttons felt clumsy, until we discovered Figma Make, its built-in AI assistant. By writing descriptive prompts, the AI started helping us shape our first prototype. It took endless trial and error, but that process taught us the essence of design iteration.

After class, Jana cooked us her Hungarian specialty — potato gulyás — which instantly became one of my favourite meals of the whole trip. We spent the evening eating, laughing, and talking about how different Spain felt from the Netherlands.

Thursday was pure focus. We had to finalise our first dashboard prototype by Monday, so I handled the main interface layout with Georgi, while Jana and Carme developed the user profiles. Setting up the shared Figma education workspace for everyone was tricky, but we managed. Although our team worked well, we had ongoing struggles with Tami, who tended to isolate himself and focus on side projects. It took patience and several group check-ins to rebuild trust, but by the end of the module he had re-engaged with us.

Friday brought one of the most memorable experiences: our excursion to Aqualia, the local water-treatment plant. Toni and Rosa met us early at 8:30 and guided us through the facility. The engineers explained how they collect and process water-quality data using IoT sensors and solar-powered systems. I was fascinated by how precisely they managed electricity usage during peak solar hours. Seeing sensors in real life — devices doing what we had only discussed conceptually — made our project feel tangible and relevant.

After the visit, Jana and Alexa joined the South African group for lunch at our apartment. We told Jana we missed pannekoek, so she made her Austrian version — thin, sweet pancakes with cinnamon and Nutella — and it tasted exactly like home. Later, Rourke let Alexa braid his hair, which ended in tears of laughter.

That evening, we dressed up for a local coffee party (which turned out to be a night-time open-air bar serving espresso martinis). We ordered a Tesla as our Uber — something I still brag about — and danced for hours even though we didn’t understand a word of the lyrics. It was one of those spontaneous, joy-filled nights that make the friendships in this program feel like family.

Cultural Awareness

By the end of Week 1, I had fallen in love with Spain’s warmth — both literal and emotional. The people of Lleida live slowly and connect deeply. Meals last for hours, conversations stretch into laughter, and time feels flexible. The language barrier forced me to adapt; every order at a café was a mini-performance, but it built confidence.

Our group dynamic evolved too. We met frequently with Jana, Georgi and Tobias for evening walks, snacks, and sometimes just to talk about life. Those quiet strolls through the narrow streets or up to the castle created a rhythm of reflection. We weren’t just classmates — we were becoming a global family.

On Saturday, the whole cohort became tourists for a day and travelled to Barcelona. Alexa, fluent in Spanish, naturally became our guide. Walking out of the metro, I froze — in front of me rose the Basilica de la Sagrada Família, towering like it touched the clouds. We toured its breathtaking interior, bathed in rainbow light from stained glass. Afterwards, we sat quietly in a church, praying together in our own ways — Christians, agnostics, believers and non-believers — all united by respect and gratitude.

Outside, a group of locals performed a traditional Catalan dance. Alexa and I jumped in to join them, learning the steps as the crowd clapped along. Later, I entertained some kids by twisting balloon animals, which drew laughter from strangers. We ended the day eating real paella (as Alexa insisted), swimming at the beach, sipping sangria, and riding carnival attractions with Tobias. It was, without question, the best day in Spain.

Sunday was slower. A few of us visited Castell de la Suda again, this time for dinner at the hilltop restaurant, followed by board games at the apartment. Tobias taught us a murder-mystery card game that became our weekly ritual. These small traditions — walks, food, laughter — made Lleida feel like home.

Reflection – What? So What? Now What?
What?

Week 1 was filled with discovery. I learned the foundations of UX Design — empathy, accessibility, and user-centred thinking — and started applying them in our prototype. Beyond the classroom, I built friendships, experienced Spanish culture, and saw technology’s real-world impact during the Aqualia visit.

So What?

This week changed how I define “design.” It’s not lines and colours; it’s people, culture, rhythm, and emotion. Spain taught me that creativity grows in warm spaces — in shared meals, laughter, and late-night conversations. Even small frustrations, like language barriers or team tension, became lessons in patience and empathy.

Now What?

Going forward, I’ll keep combining empathy with structure — designing not just for efficiency, but for connection. The balance of focus and joy I found in Lleida will shape every project I take on: serious about goals, but never forgetting the people behind the screen.

Week 2 – UX Design in Lleida: Testing, Feedback & Finding Balance

Null-Measurement

After the first intense week of new faces, travel and culture, Week 2 started with more structure. I already knew the core principles of UX from Toni’s lectures—user-centred design, empathy, accessibility—but I still needed to learn how to conduct a formal usability test, collect data, and interpret feedback in a disciplined way.

I was confident about visual design and Figma, but less sure about analysing results or running testing software such as RUXAILAB, which was completely new to me. I also knew that this week would test my time management: finishing our prototype while keeping up with reflections, the Belgium Campus online career day, and, well, surviving on limited sleep and coffee.

Project Contribution

At the start of the week, Toni and Rosa briefed us on the structure of our UX deliverables and what our next deadline would include. Our group immediately began improving the Hartbeespoort Dam dashboard prototype. Georgi and I refined the map view—adjusting spacing, colour contrasts and icon alignment—while Jana and Carme worked on the data panels and filters.

We implemented Toni’s feedback from last week, ensuring consistency and applying the 10 Usability Heuristics we’d learned: visibility of system status, user control, and recognition over recall. I spent hours fixing transitions between pages and making the dashboard smoother.

Marc joined the class this week to teach us RUXAILAB, the tool we would use to host our online usability tests. Setting it up took patience; the interface was in Spanish, and every field needed careful translation. Once running, we created user scenarios and uploaded our Figma prototype. I helped write the testing script: tasks such as “find a sensor reading,” “download a report,” and “use the filter to locate a pollution alert.”

On Wednesday, Toni held the in-class usability testing session. Unfortunately, the South African group—including me—had to miss it because of our Belgium Campus career day. The rest of the team tested with Alec’s group and uploaded the results for us. When I opened the feedback document later that night, my stomach dropped—it was long and brutally honest. Still, I realised that this was the real UX process: people struggling meant our design had blind spots we hadn’t seen.

Thursday and Friday became redesign marathons. We gathered in the apartment living room with laptops, Kinder Joys and background music, going line-by-line through every comment. I handled the filter system, clarified the export process, and made sure error messages were readable. Jana reworked the layout; Georgi optimised button logic. We re-tested internally and even sent the link to a few outside friends to get fresh reactions. By the end of Friday night, our updated version looked cleaner and felt intuitive.

Somewhere in those long hours I developed a Kinder Joy obsession—I’d bought so many trying to collect the Stranger Things figurines that it became a running joke. Between edits, Georgi and I would open one just to see what toy we got; it was ridiculous but hilarious, and it kept spirits high.

Cultural Awareness

Even while the week revolved around testing, we didn’t lose our Spanish rhythm. On Tuesday evening, I cooked melkkos, or “pannekoek pap,” for everyone. It’s a warm, creamy dish from home, and to my surprise everyone loved it—especially Jana, who said it was perfect “comfort food after debugging.” That dinner made me feel closer to the group; food always does that.

Later that night, we kept our murder-mystery card-game tradition with Jana, Georgi and Tobias. Those late-night rounds became our way of decompressing after long technical days, laughing until midnight.

On Sunday, Marco, Musti, Rourke and I finally toured the castle interior we had only seen from outside the week before. The echoing halls and narrow staircases felt straight out of Harry Potter; I joked that I’d found my Hogwarts. Learning about the castle’s medieval history added a sense of place that grounded me beyond the classroom.

Through all these moments, I noticed how naturally we had blended cultures. English mixed with bits of Spanish and Afrikaans; we’d correct each other’s pronunciations, share idioms, and always end up laughing. Lleida didn’t feel foreign anymore—it felt lived-in.

Reflection – What? So What? Now What?
What?

This week revolved around usability testing and iteration. I helped finalise our prototype, set up and interpret RUXAILAB tests, and implement design fixes from peer and user feedback. We also managed Belgium Campus obligations, kept up our social bonds, and explored Lleida further.

So What?

I learned that criticism is the designer’s best friend. Seeing people struggle with my interface hurt at first, but it transformed how I see testing: it’s not failure—it’s direction. I also learned that cultural rituals—shared meals, jokes, and downtime—hold teams together under stress. My Kinder Joy addiction even became a team inside-joke that broke tension when things got too serious.

Now What?

Moving forward, I’ll keep applying feedback loops early and often, and I’ll document each design change with a clear reason “why.” Personally, I’ll remember that good UX design is as much about people and empathy as it is about buttons and layouts. Week 2 taught me that balance—work intensely, laugh often, and celebrate the small wins along the way.

Week 3 – Analytics, CIF Report, Major Revamp & Final Presentation

Null-Measurement

When Week 3 began, there was a strange mix of pressure and calm in the air. I’d already learned how to design and test a dashboard, but I hadn’t yet turned raw usability results into analytical data and structured documentation. Toni introduced the CIF (Common Industry Format) for reporting test results — something I had never used before — and it felt like the missing piece between creativity and professionalism.

My goal for the final week was simple but meaningful: to wrap everything up properly. I wanted our dashboard to reflect the feedback we had received, to document it neatly in the CIF, and to leave Spain knowing that I had grown not just as a designer, but as a communicator and teammate.

Project Contribution

We started the week with Toni walking us through how to analyse our test results. This time, instead of just looking at what didn’t work, we were learning why it didn’t work and how to show that in a measurable way. As we went through the data, I could already see patterns forming — users hesitated at certain icons, misunderstood colour codes, or got lost in the filter options.

After class, our team — Four Elements — sat together to plan our final sprint. We divided the work evenly: everyone had a piece to focus on. The mood was calm but productive. Even Tami, who had been distant before, jumped in and took initiative, which really lifted the team’s spirit.

This day was intense but rewarding. Toni gave us the official structure for the CIF report, and we immediately got to work. I paired with Georgi to handle the Errors and Improvements section, while Jana and Carme finalised visuals and documentation.

As we reviewed our prototype, we realised something huge — the entire theme needed a refresh. The interface had become cluttered over time, and our colour contrasts weren’t as clear as they could be. Instead of patching it piece by piece, we decided to rebuild the layout and style from scratch.

It was a long process, but it brought new energy into the group. We adjusted spacing, changed font hierarchy, redefined colours for accessibility, and most importantly, added the missing features that tied everything together — AI analytics, recommendations, and a “problem summary” panel that would help decision-makers at a glance.

By the end of the day, the dashboard felt cohesive again — modern, clean, and genuinely useful. Toni walked past, saw our screen, and simply said, “Now this looks like a product.” That one sentence made the exhaustion worth it.

There were no classes scheduled, which gave us the whole day to finalise our CIF report and prepare for the presentation. Since most of the work was done, the atmosphere was lighthearted.

We decided to invite Marc and Jana over to our apartment for lunch. Musti cooked a proper South African Bunny Chow — thick curry in a hollowed-out bread loaf. The food turned out so spicy that everyone had tears in their eyes, but they loved it. The laughter around that tiny kitchen table was one of my favourite memories from Spain.

Later that afternoon, we did an escape room at Escapat Lleida. The theme was “Wild West.” One Austrian, one Spaniard, and four South Africans locked in a room, solving riddles and searching for a gun to save the sheriff. The teamwork was effortless — communication flowed naturally, everyone contributed, and we escaped in just under 50 minutes, one of the best times of the day. It felt like a perfect metaphor for our group: chaotic at first, but unstoppable once we clicked.

That evening, Tobias, Georgi and Tami joined us as we returned to the apartment for a relaxed poker night, followed by our signature murder mystery card game. It had become such a tradition that no week felt complete without it. Around 1 a.m., we took a spontaneous walk to the castle, climbing up the quiet streets with music playing softly from someone’s phone. Standing there overlooking the glowing city, it hit me how much Lleida had started to feel like home.

The final day came faster than expected. We dressed up for our UX Design presentation and walked to the university with coffee in hand. Each team presented their project, reflecting on three weeks of work. When our turn came, we confidently explained our journey: from initial research and testing, through redesign and analytics, to the finished CIF report.

Toni smiled throughout our talk and, at the end, surprised the entire class with packets of Spanish candy as a farewell gift. It was such a small gesture but perfectly summed up his personality — supportive, kind, and proud of his students.

After class, we had one last toast with our Spanish friends at a nearby café. There was laughter, hugs, and the bittersweet feeling that always comes with goodbyes.

The next morning, we woke up early, packed our bags, and headed to the train station. It was quiet — everyone lost in thought. On the way to Barcelona Airport, I stared out the window at the hills fading into the distance and thought about how much we’d grown here. Lleida had started as a place on a map; now it was part of my story.

Cultural Awareness

Looking back, Week 3 was the perfect blend of professional focus and personal closure. We weren’t just ticking boxes — we were living through the work.

The escape room reminded me how natural teamwork can become when people from different cultures truly listen to each other. Cooking Bunny Chow showed me that sharing food is the easiest way to connect worlds. And that 1 a.m. castle walk — our final adventure — captured exactly what this program is about: exploration, friendship, and wonder.

Even as we prepared for the next destination, I noticed how much I had changed. My confidence in collaboration had grown. I had learned to express ideas clearly across languages and personalities. Lleida had taught me patience, empathy, and the beauty of slow living — that sometimes the best work happens when you take a moment to breathe and look around.

Reflection – What? So What? Now What?
What?

We finalised our UX dashboard, documented results in the CIF report, redesigned the interface completely, and added analytical features like AI recommendations. We wrapped up with a polished presentation and meaningful closure through social experiences — Bunny Chow lunch, escape room, poker and murder mystery nights, and a final castle walk before heading to Austria.

So What?

I learned that design is never “done” — it just evolves. The CIF report taught me how to translate usability into measurable value, and our redesign proved that sometimes you need to start fresh to get it right. Personally, I learned how culture and friendship fuel teamwork. The laughter and shared meals balanced the workload, turning stress into something deeply human.

Now What?

As I move into the next module in Austria, I want to carry this balance with me — structure and heart, analytics and empathy. Spain showed me that a great designer doesn’t just think about how users interact with a product, but how people interact with each other. That, to me, is real UX.
